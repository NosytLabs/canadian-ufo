Archival index · 1,510 descriptions · 59 queries · 4 record groups

The whole index, in one table

Library and Archives Canada holds roughly 9,500 digitized documents on UFOs, accumulated between 1947 and the early 1980s by four federal bodies: the Department of National Defence, the Department of Transport, the National Research Council and the RCMP. What follows is that database's own index — 1,510 descriptions, sortable, with a direct link into the live record for every entry.

This is an index, not the documents. Every entry carries a description and a record number; 1,357 of them also carry a document date and 1,311 name a location. Sighting dates are free text and are mostly absent, which is LAC's own caveat rather than a limit of this scrape.

01

What the four record groups are

Each is a different institution with a different reason to be writing things down.

National Defence

552 descriptions, the largest group, filed as “Intelligence: Sighting of unknown objects” and cross-referenced to microfilm reel T-3291. Fonds ISN 13019. Almost every province-by-group query lands here, which is why this group grew the most.

552 indexed

Transport

52 descriptions, filed under “Transportation: General: Unidentified Flying Objects”. Civil aviation and marine reporting. Fonds ISN 37061.

52 indexed

National Research Council

396 descriptions held by the Herzberg Institute of Astrophysics as “Reports on non-meteoric sightings”. The scientific file, kept after the 1967 transfer of responsibility. Fonds ISN 1017 / 4693.

RCMP

510 descriptions recorded as national research requests routed through police files, often citing the Radio and Electrical Engineering Division. Fonds ISN 37024.

02

Why this index exists at all

Library and Archives Canada's UFO database is public, searchable and, for most researchers, unusable as it stands. This is the specific reason.

The cap

Every query returns at most 50 rows, whatever the result set. There is no total count anywhere on the page, so the interface gives you no way to know how much you are not seeing.

The broken pagination

The sk parameter looks like an offset and it is not. Page two jumps to the last fifty records, and every page after that returns those same fifty records again. Paging through the National Research Council file yields fifty-one distinct records and then repeats forever.

The workaround

The only way deeper is to vary the query. AURORA runs 59 of them: four record groups, eleven provinces, and all forty-four province-by-group pairings, then takes the union. That turns 207 visible records into 1,510.

A single query returns the first fifty rows and the last fifty rows, and nothing in between. The archive was never incomplete. It was just not reachable through the interface that describes it.
Measured against the live database, September 2026 · reproducible with the query patterns below

What this means for the numbers on this site. 1,510 is a floor, not a total. Every description LAC holds that can be reached by varying the query is here, and the collection itself is documented at roughly 9,500 documents. The gap between those two numbers is not lost data in any meaningful sense; it is the part of the collection that no combination of the interface's own query fields exposes.

What the index is honest about. Of 1,510 descriptions, 1,357 carry a document date and 1,311 name a location. Sighting dates are free text and largely absent, which is LAC's own caveat, not an artefact of this scrape. Where a field is empty, the table says so rather than guessing.

One record, found this way

The province query for Nova Scotia returns a description that the record-group query for National Defence never shows: an intelligence file whose location field reads Shelburne County, NS and whose microfilm is reel T-3291. Shelburne was the site of a United States naval submarine-tracking installation, and the object in question is the one that entered the water off Shag Harbour six kilometres away on 4 October 1967.

That record is invisible to anyone who queries by department alone. It is here because the scraper asked about Nova Scotia as well as about Defence.

04

Query the live database yourself

The LAC database is public and browsable. These are the query patterns that work — by department, or by province.

  • Browse by record group: National Research Council
    ?q7=National+Research+Council sets the record group. Also works for Department of National Defence, Department of Transport, Royal Canadian Mounted Police.
  • Browse by province: New Brunswick
    ?q4=NB sets the province or territory. NB, NS, NL, PE, MB, ON, QC, BC, YT all return records.
  • Browse by province — Newfoundland and Labrador
    ?q4=Nfld, not ?q4=NL. LAC's province codes are their own: Newfoundland is Nfld and Prince Edward Island is PEI, and the standard abbreviations return nothing.
  • Browse by province and record group together
    ?q4=NB&q7=Royal+Canadian+Mounted+Police — the pairing that reaches records no single query returns. This is the shape of all forty-four matrix queries behind this index.
  • Curated guide — 1967 Shag Harbour sighting and related research
    LAC's own research guide assembles the Shag Harbour material, with volume and microfilm references.. This is the single best starting point for a specific case.
  • LAC scope page — Canada's UFOs: the search for the unknown
    Confirms four origin departments, 1947 to the early 1980s, ~9,500 digitized documents, and states plainly that dates are unstandardised and only about half the records name a location.
